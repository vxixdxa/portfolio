## Overview

Designed the multi-role permissions, contact management, group communication, and business upgrade experience for an enterprise communication platform — lowering the operational cost of complex enterprise communication scenarios through clear information architecture and a scalable UI system.

---

## The Challenge

66 Chat is an enterprise communication platform that needs to support different roles — frontline employees, managers, and system administrators — while also offering business modules that let organizations expand functionality to fit their own needs.

The core challenge of this project was this: how to keep a multi-role, multi-permission, multi-module product architecture clear, and prevent users from getting confused as they move between daily communication, team management, and system settings.

### Core Pain Points

- Different roles hold different permissions and tasks, but the original structure made it easy for users to lose track of which actions they could actually perform.
- Contacts, groups, and labels were classified across many levels, so users spent more time finding people or managing lists.
- Business features and upgrade plans weren't woven naturally into the usage context, making it hard for users to grasp the value of advanced features.
- The platform needed to convey the stability and trust of an enterprise-grade product while staying comfortable for long hours of daily use.

---

## My Role & Responsibilities

I led product experience design, information architecture, role-and-permission logic, interface flows, UI visual design, and design system planning. Throughout the process, I focused on translating complex enterprise communication logic into a clear, understandable, and scalable user experience.

---

## Design Strategy

### Building a Role-Oriented Interface Structure

I reorganized user roles and feature permissions so that different roles see content suited to their tasks within the same product framework. Regular employees focus on chat and contacts; managers can go further to manage teams and group communication; admins handle organization settings, permissions, and advanced features.

This avoids building entirely separate systems for each role, and means users don't have to relearn the whole interface when their role changes.

### Reducing Cognitive Load with Progressive Disclosure

Rather than exposing every feature at once, I revealed them gradually based on the user's role, context, and needs. Core features stay consistent while advanced ones appear when needed, keeping the interface simple while preserving the product's depth.

This lets new users get started quickly, while advanced users can complete more complex tasks within a familiar structure.

### Restructuring Contact & Group Management

For contacts, groups, and labels, I rethought the classification model and entry points so users can more intuitively find people, manage lists, and carry out group communication.

The focus wasn't on adding more categories, but on making the classification logic closer to everyday communication scenarios — reducing the effort of finding, filtering, and managing contacts.

### Weaving Business Upgrades into Context

I shifted business upgrades from a plain feature-restriction page to contextual feature guidance. When users hit an advanced need during real use, the interface surfaces that feature's value, use case, and preview state, helping users understand what problem upgrading would solve.

This makes upgrading not just a payment gateway, but part of the product experience.

---

## Impact

- Made multi-role permissions and enterprise communication flows clearer, reducing confusion around role switching and feature use.
- Reorganized the contacts, groups, and labels structure to make everyday communication and list management more intuitive.
- Through contextual guidance, made the value of business features easier to understand.
- Built a scalable UI architecture that supports future extension of roles, permissions, and business modules.
